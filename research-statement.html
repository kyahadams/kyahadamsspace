Research statement

Space as a technical system and an institutional one.

Perspective

The questions I care about begin where technical capability meets collective consequence.

Space systems operate inside physical environments, markets, institutions, and governance regimes. I am interested in the points where those layers collide.

My background in political science, history, and sociology trained me to think about institutions and collective action. Graduate work in space operations and research at XDLab moved me toward the computational side of the same problems: orbital population evolution, debris risk, carrying capacity, and model-based decision support.

Current work

From orbital models to decision-relevant evidence.

I use MOCAT-MC and related tools to study orbital sustainability. My work includes scenario design, population initialization, launch-data ingestion, and interpretation of modeled outcomes.

Two projects define my current direction. My orbital carrying-capacity research connects physical limits in LEO with economic and access consequences. Catalyst examines how large language models can translate simulation outputs into policy-facing reasoning while preserving uncertainty and assumptions.

The model output is not the end of the research problem. The next question is how institutions interpret and act on it.

Doctoral direction

Adaptive governance for computationally complex systems.

In doctoral work, I want to study how technical models and AI systems can support governance without becoming opaque substitutes for judgment. Space is a compelling domain because it combines measurable physical constraints, shared-resource dynamics, commercial growth, international institutions, and high uncertainty.

I am especially interested in operationalizing carrying-capacity concepts, representing uncertainty in policy-facing simulation, comparing how AI systems interpret identical evidence, and designing governance mechanisms that can adapt as the underlying technical environment changes.
